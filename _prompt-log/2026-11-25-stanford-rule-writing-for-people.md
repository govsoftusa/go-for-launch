## Supplied guidance

Writing for people

Content that reads like an AI wrote it for another AI is a defect. It happens for four reasons, and the process below exists to prevent each one:
1. The writer writes from its brief, not from the reader's world. The instructions, the site map and the writer's own rules leak into the copy ("Find the page written for the work you do", "we'll be straight about what isn't built").
2. There is no real material. Without the customer's own words, real situations and checked facts, the gaps get filled with abstractions and lists of personas.
3. Style rules turn into templates. A rule followed mechanically becomes a new repeated pattern, such as every paragraph opening with "If you're…" and a bold lead-in.
4. The writer reviews its own work. The agent that wrote the copy can't see its own patterns.

The process

Follow these steps for any content a person will read: website and product copy, emails, docs, designs, messages and announcements.
1. Write a brief first, outside the content. Name the reader as one real person: their role, the moment they arrive and what they already believe. Say what they should know or do afterwards. List the evidence you have: product facts with their source, the customer's own language and real examples. The brief stays in your notes or the project's reference files. It never appears in the copy.
2. Gather real language before drafting. Use meeting notes, call transcripts, support threads, the customer's documents and the owner's own words. Borrow their nouns and verbs. If you have no real material for a claim or a scenario, don't write it: ask, or mark the gap for the owner.
3. Draft from the reader's world. Open with something concrete from their week: a deal, a document, a deadline, a person. A headline states something the reader cares about, never a category or a description of the page.
4. Strip the scaffolding. Delete every sentence about the page, the site, the navigation, your process or your rules. If a sentence only makes sense to someone who read your brief, cut it.
5. Get a review from fresh eyes. Use a separate agent or a person who did not write the draft, give them only the copy and the source facts, and have them do three things:
	- mark every tell in the list below;
	- mark every sentence someone at the company wouldn't say to a customer across a table;
	- check every claim against its source.
6. Revise, then compare across pages. Read the page next to its neighbors. If the same sentence frame, section shape or opener repeats, change it.

Tells to reject on sight

- Meta copy: text about the page, site or process. Examples: "Find the page written for…", "Pick the one closest to you", "Here's what that means", "This page covers", "We'll show you…".
- Instruction leakage: narrating your own rules or virtues. Examples: "we'll be straight about what isn't built", "in plain words", "no jargon", "to be honest". Be honest instead: state a status once, where it matters.
- Persona roll calls: a list of audiences in place of one reader. Example: "A CFO, a banker, a CIO and a compliance officer all use X". It is usually also an unsupported claim about customers.
- Formula rhythm: stock contrasts and forced symmetry. Examples: "not X, but Y", "but not for the same reasons", "whether you're X or Y", "from X to Y", triads by default, every sentence the same length.
- Repeated frames: one opener or structure used for every item ("If you're a…", a bold lead-in plus one sentence, a heading plus a one-liner plus bullets). Use a frame at most twice on a page.
- Labels in place of meaning:
	- a kicker that repeats the menu item or the heading;
	- icon rows or badges used as decoration;
	- all-caps labels and step codes (01 · FIND, RAISES);
	- caption-style data lines ("Records in X, October 26, 2026.");
	- a status table or changelog at the top of a page.
- Abstract nouns where a concrete one exists: "the work you do", "what changes", "the capital side", "solutions", "workflows", "use cases".
- Inventory fragments and noun stacks: "Sign-in invitations with delivery and bounce status", "Members, seats and requests to join".
- Implementation detail: rate limits, protocol names, field names, file retention, row caps and where a check runs. If a limit matters to a buyer, say it once, in plain words, where they choose a plan.
- Filler: "seamless", "robust", "leverage", "unlock", "empower", "streamline", "cutting-edge", "in today's fast-paced world".

Rules that still apply

- Write to one reader as "you", and say "we" for the company. Use full sentences with plain verbs.
- Say why something matters to the reader, not only that it exists. One clear benefit beats three listed features.
- Use as many items as the content has, not three by default. Vary sentence length and structure.
- Stay honest while sounding human. Never imply that unbuilt features work, never invent customers, quotes or numbers, and never drop a placeholder that still needs a real answer.

Before you hand it over

- The brief exists, and every claim in the copy traces to a source in it.
- No sentence describes the page, the site, your process or your rules.
- A reviewer who didn't write it has marked the tells, and you fixed them.
- Read aloud, every sentence sounds like someone at the company talking to a customer.
- No opener or structure repeats more than twice on the page, and neighboring pages don't share the same shape.
